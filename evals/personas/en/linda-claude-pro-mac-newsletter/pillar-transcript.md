Question 1 of 5. Tell me about the first time you wrote your own Tuesday. Where were you, and what was on the page?

**Linda:** Okay. I'm going to ramble, stop me. It was August 2024, near the end of the garage, the garage was basically done, there was nothing left to label. And it was a Tuesday, actually, which I didn't think about until later. I was at the kitchen table at like 6:30 in the morning. Tom was still asleep. I'd been reading one of those books about finding your purpose, the kind with a sunset on the cover, and it had a chapter with 40 questions in it. Forty. And I got to question 3 and I closed it. I couldn't. Every question was "what would you do if you could not fail" and I thought, I don't know, I'd probably still not know what to do.

And there was an envelope on the table from HR, from the company, it was the paperwork for the package, and I turned it over and I just started writing on the back of it. Not what's my purpose. Just, what does a normal Tuesday look like that I would actually want. And I wrote: up at 6:30, not 5:15. Coffee on the porch, not in the car. Before lunch, one real conversation with one person about their work, not a meeting, a conversation. Paid for something I'm actually good at, writing or listening. Home by 4 on Thursdays for Mom. And then the last line, and I remember underlining it, nobody else's emails.

And I read it back and I cried a little, honestly, because it was so small. I thought, that's it? Twenty-six years and that's what I want? And then I looked at it again and I realized I had already trained for it. The coaching. One conversation with one person about their life. I'd done 14 of those for free and loved every one of them, and I'd been sitting in a garage for four months not seeing it.

I showed Tom when he came down. He read it with his coffee and he said "that's it?" And I said that's it. And he said "well, that seems doable." Which is the most Tom thing anyone has ever said. But he was right. That's the point. A dream isn't doable. A Tuesday is.

The envelope is still in the kitchen drawer. The one with the takeout menus. I take it out sometimes.

**Machine:** Got it. Next: when you ask a client to write a Tuesday instead of a big vision, what do they say back? Their exact words, if you remember them.

**Linda:** Oh, they push back. Almost everybody. The first thing is "that sounds boring." Like I've insulted them. And I get it, because everybody they've talked to so far has said think big, dream big, what's your vision. And I come along and say tell me about Tuesday.

Joan said, and I wrote this down, "I've had 31 years of Tuesdays, Linda." Like, why would I want another one. And I said, you've had 31 years of somebody else's Tuesdays. You've never written one. And she was quiet for a while and then she said "huh." Joan doesn't say "huh" lightly.

Renée tried to negotiate. She said "can I write a Saturday instead?" And I said no, absolutely not. Anybody can write a nice Saturday. Saturday you're at the farmers market, you're happy, everyone's happy. Saturday doesn't tell you anything. Tuesday is the day you can't fake. Tuesday is the day you find out whether you'd still want it when nothing special is happening. That's the test.

The other thing they say, and this is the one that breaks my heart a little, is "I don't want small. I've been small." Because they feel like they were made small at the end. Restructured, consolidated, packaged out. And I tell them, a Tuesday isn't small. A Tuesday is specific. Those aren't the same thing. The vision is the thing that's actually small, because it fits on a corkboard and it never has to meet your real life.

And I'll say this, because it's true for me too. Underneath "that sounds boring" is usually "I'm scared that if I write it down and it's ordinary, then the 26 years or the 31 years were for nothing." And they weren't. You can't write a good Tuesday without the 26 years. You need them to know what you're done with.

**Machine:** Got it. Next: tell me about a time you watched the dream way, the vision board or the big plan, not work.

**Linda:** Patty. My friend Patty. I love her, I've known her since our boys were in kindergarten. She left a big job at an insurance company, a little before me actually, and the first thing she did, the very first week, she made a vision board. She invited me over to help. We had wine, we cut up magazines. It was a nice evening, I'm not going to pretend it wasn't.

And on it there's a picture of Tuscany, a stone house with the shutters, and a woman in linen pants on a dock somewhere, which, I don't know why there's a dock in Tuscany, and the word BREATHE cut out of a magazine in big letters. And it went up in her laundry room. And it is still there. Two years. She said to me last month, she said "I see it every time I fold towels." And she hasn't been to Tuscany and she hasn't changed one single thing about her week. She's not unhappy exactly. She's waiting. She's been waiting for two years for the board to do something.

Nobody ever moved forward because of a corkboard. I say that and people laugh, but I mean it. The board tells you where you'd like to end up. It never tells you what to do at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday.

And there's the retreat version. A woman from my coach training, I won't say her name, she went on a 5-day retreat in the desert after she left her job. Yoga, journaling, a sound bath, whatever that is. She came home on a Sunday night and she called me, she said "I feel so clear." And on Monday morning she was sitting at her kitchen table answering emails for a volunteer board she didn't even like. Same kitchen. Same emails. The clarity lasted about a day and a half.

And honestly half of it, the not knowing, is that nobody's sleeping. The 3 a.m. thing, the hot flashes, I could do a whole hour on how you can't decide anything when you've been awake since 3. I really could. Park that. Park that, that's a different thing. Where was I.

The dream way, the problem is it's all destination and no Tuesday. You can't take a step toward Tuscany on a Tuesday morning. You can take a step toward one conversation before lunch.

**Machine:** Got it. Next: tell me about a client whose Tuesday surprised them, or surprised you.

**Linda:** Joan. Joan surprised both of us. When we started, she told me she figured her Tuesday would be travel. She'd always said, when I retire I'm going to travel. So I thought, okay, we'll see. And she wrote it, and it took her two sessions, she kept crossing things out, and the Tuesday she finally read to me had nothing about travel in it at all.

It was: up at 7, walk Biscuit, Biscuit is her dog, a very fat beagle. At a table with other people by 9. Needed by somebody before noon. Home by 3 to walk Biscuit again. No spreadsheets. Ever. And she read the line "needed by somebody before noon" and she stopped, and she said "oh. That's what I miss." Not the job. Being needed before noon. For 31 years people needed her numbers by 10 a.m. and she hated the numbers but she loved being needed.

So then it's easy, almost. You look at that Tuesday and you ask, where do people need somebody like Joan before noon, 3 days a week, with no spreadsheets? And that's how she ended up at the community foundation running their volunteer programs, 3 days a week. About 4 months after our first call. She says "I'm needed before noon again." She also says Biscuit has never been fitter.

Carol's surprised me more, honestly. Carol was a regional sales director for 28 years and I thought she'd write something big, she's a big personality. And her Tuesday was tiny. No meetings. Not fewer meetings. None. And one line that said "be the best at something small." And there was a whiteboard in it. She wrote, a room with 6 new people in it and a whiteboard, and I'm teaching them how to actually listen to a customer.

And that's what she does now. She trains new sales reps 2 days a week for a little family-owned distributor. She gets paid. She has a whiteboard. She sent me a picture of it. She decided in 9 weeks that she's not going back to full time anything, and some people would call that not a result, and I think it's the best result, because she knows. She knows what she's for.

The thing that surprises people is that the Tuesday is never what they expected. It's almost always smaller and more specific than the dream, and it's almost always closer to something they were already good at.

**Machine:** Got it. Last one: what's the smallest step someone could take this week, on their own?

**Linda:** Tonight, or tomorrow morning before anyone's up. Twenty minutes. A pencil, not the laptop, the laptop makes you edit. The back of an envelope is perfect, honestly, it's the right size, it stops you from writing a novel.

And you write one ordinary Tuesday, two years from now. Not the best day. A normal one. And you answer 5 things. What time do you get up. Who do you talk to before lunch. What do you get paid for, if anything. What don't you do anymore. And where are you at 3 o'clock. That's it. Five lines.

Then the part everybody skips. Read it to one person. Out loud. Not post it, not put it on LinkedIn. Read it to your sister, your husband, your neighbor over the fence. Because when you say it out loud you find out which line you believe and which line you wrote because it sounded nice. You'll hear it in your own voice. The fake line always sounds like a brochure.

And I'll say this. If it scares you a little because it's so small, good. That means it's real. You can't write a Tuesday while you're still wearing the badge in your head, so if you notice the badge sneaking in, if your Tuesday has a title in it, cross the title out. Tuesday doesn't care what your title was.

And if anyone does it, I'd love to read it. Just reply and send it to me. I read every one.
