Question 1 of 5. Tell me about the first time you found out a client everyone loved was losing money. Where were you?

**Erin:** Okay. So everybody thinks my story starts in the stairwell, and it kind of does, but the first time I actually saw it was 2 years earlier. 2017. And it was pizza.

I was controller then. Every Monday I approved the expense reports, and every Monday there were pizza receipts. Saturday pizza, Sunday pizza, from the same place around the corner, the one with the red awning. And I asked our office manager, who is ordering all this pizza on the weekends? And she said, "Oh, that's the retail team. They're always in on weekends for that account." One account.

So I got curious, which is a dangerous thing for an accountant. I started a little spreadsheet on the side. I called it the pizza ledger. Every weekend receipt, every overtime hour for the hourly people, every freelancer we brought in on a Friday night because the deadline moved again. For one client. I did it for about 3 months, on my own time, nobody asked me to.

And at the end of it, that client, the one we put in every new business deck, the one the CEO talked about at the holiday party, was costing us money. Once you put the real hours in, the weekends, the freelancers, the pizza, it was underwater.

So I walked it into the CEO's office. I printed it, because it was 2017 and I still printed things. And he looked at it for maybe a minute, and he slid it back across the desk and he said, "Erin, they're our logo. You don't fire the logo." And that was the end of the meeting.

And we kept them. And 2 years later I'm in the stairwell calling the bank. Different client by then, the restaurant account, same exact problem.

So the lesson I took out of that office wasn't about pizza. It was that nobody wanted to know. The numbers were right there. You don't order that much pizza for one client and not know. Everybody kind of knows. Nobody wants to know. And because nobody had ever looked at it one client at a time, nobody had to do anything about it.

That's honestly the whole reason I start every sprint with the client by client. Not the forecast, not pricing. First we look. Because until you look, it's a feeling, and you can argue with a feeling all day long. You can't argue with a client-level P and L. I mean, you can. People try. But it's harder.

**Machine:** Got it. Next: walk me through how you check one client. What do you look at first?

**Erin:** Okay, this is the part I love, the accountant part. I'll keep it short, I promise.

First, what did they actually pay us. Not what's on the contract, what came in, for the last 12 months. And you take out the pass-throughs. Media spend, freelancers you billed straight through, printing, all of it. A paid media agency can look like a giant client that's mostly ad spend just passing through your bank account. That's not your revenue. That's their money taking a walk through your building.

Second, the hours. Start with the timesheets, whatever's in them. Then the part nobody wants to do: the hours that don't get logged. Every agency has them. Friday afternoon, the "quick call" that's 50 minutes, the Sunday email the account lead answers from her kitchen. So I ask every account lead the same question. If this client disappeared tomorrow, what would your Fridays look like? And you can watch their face. If the answer is "I'd have my Fridays back," that client has hours that aren't on any timesheet.

Third, what those hours actually cost. Loaded. Not the salary. Salary plus payroll taxes plus benefits plus a fair share of the rent and the software. People always forget the loaded part and then wonder why the math doesn't work.

And then it's subtraction. What they paid, minus pass-throughs, minus the loaded hours. That's the delivery margin for that one client. Then you do it for every client and you sort the list. The bottom 2 or 3 is where the sprint goes.

And there's a quick version I give people who aren't ready for any of that. The Monday meeting test. Which client's name comes up the most in your Monday meeting? Just count it. Nine times out of — okay, I don't have that number, don't write that down. But a lot of the time the loudest client and the underwater client are the same client.

People always ask me here about how to allocate overhead, and whether you should run it by entity, and what it does to their taxes, and that's a whole... okay, park that. Park that. That's a different day, and the tax part is their tax person, not me.

One more thing. You do this for the last 12 months, not the last month. One month lies. One month has a big invoice or a dead week. Twelve months tells the truth.

They cut the scope to a fixed monthly package with a list of what's in and what's out, and the "quick favors" went on the out list. You can keep a client you love on purpose. **Machine:** Got it. Last one: what would you say to the agency owner reading this who's scared to look?

**Erin:** I'd say, I know. I know you're scared to look. I was scared to walk that pizza ledger into the CEO's office, and it wasn't even my agency.

But here's the math. The number is already there. It's sitting in your timesheets and your bank account right now. Looking at it doesn't make it worse. Not looking at it is what makes it worse, because every month you don't look is another month of paying a client to be your client.

And I'd say, you don't have to do the whole thing. Don't do all your clients this week. Do one. Your biggest one. Open the timesheets, add the Friday hours nobody logs, take out the pass-throughs, and compare it to what they pay you. Twenty minutes, maybe less. If it's fine, wonderful, you'll sleep better tonight. If it's not fine, now you know, and knowing is the only place a fix starts.

And please don't fix it by growing. That's the instinct. Record year, empty account, so let's go get more revenue. If the margin is broken, more revenue is just more of the problem, faster. Margin before more. Fix the one client first, then grow.

And I'd say to them, you're not bad with money. Nobody ever showed you the view. You've been looking at revenue because revenue is the only number anybody ever put in front of you. The bank app is not a forecast and the P and L from your bookkeeper is a rearview mirror. Neither one tells you which client is eating you. That's not a character flaw. That's just a missing report.

And if you look and you find it and you don't know what to do next, that's literally my job. But honestly, most people, the minute they see the number, they already know what to do. They just needed to see it.

So. One client. This week. Look.
